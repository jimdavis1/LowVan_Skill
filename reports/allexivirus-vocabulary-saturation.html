The curve

Each point is the mean of 20 random draws of that many genomes, counting the distinct annotation strings they carry between them.

Reading it

A saturating vocabulary would bend toward a ceiling: past some sample size, new genomes would mostly reuse names already seen. This one does not bend. On a log x-axis a saturating curve flattens and a non-saturating one stays straight or steepens, and the source curve is still climbing at the right-hand edge — the last doubling of the sample adds nearly as many new strings as the one before it.

That is the argument for a controlled vocabulary stated as a measurement rather than an opinion. The green line is flat by construction: the module emits 7 annotations regardless of how many genomes it is given, because those are properties of the biology and not of who deposited the record.

The practical consequence. Anything downstream that groups proteins by their annotation string gets a different answer every time the database grows. A query for this module's proteins written against today's 88 strings will silently miss records deposited tomorrow. Against the controlled set it will not.

Caveats worth stating

  • The curve counts distinct strings, not errors. Most of the growth is orthographic — spacing, case, "putative", the trailing word "protein" — and is harmless to a human reader while being fatal to an exact-match query.
  • Sampling is by genome, so a heavily-sequenced species contributes many genomes and relatively few new strings; the curve therefore understates the diversity a broader taxon would show.
  • The 7-annotation line is fixed for this module only. The controlled vocabulary across all modules is larger, and this module adds nothing new to it.
